Why this exists · Jasmine, founder

Your body is not a transaction.

Your trust is not a closing technique. And your permission to move forward belongs entirely to you.

I know the closing playbook, so you won’t be closed.

I spent 20 years inside this industry, and I've sat on both sides of the consult table. On one side, I asked the questions, explained the options, built trust and closed the sale. On the other, I sat beside patients, in 75 consult rooms, to learn, to observe, and to find the friction and the facts.

I know how these rooms work. I know what it means when she walks in hoping to feel better about herself, trusting that the person across from her will help her make the right decision. And I know what happens when the conversation moves faster than the understanding.

I've been part of that process. I've seen what patients carry into those rooms, what they struggle to ask, and how hard it is to know what you don't know when you're the one putting your body, your trust and your money on the line.

One call stayed with me. It wasn't surgery. It was a non-surgical treatment plan, $49,510, the kind of decision that can feel lighter than it is. It made me confront the space between a patient's decision and everything she deserves to understand before making it.

I don't believe patients should have to become investigators just to feel safe asking questions. I don't believe wanting something for yourself should mean surrendering your right to understand it. And I don't believe the answer is to tell people what to choose.

The answer is to give you room to see.

What came out of those years is a vision board and a decision board that let anyone get to the truth of it: why, with whom, when, where and how.

Not to frighten you. Not to sell you something. Not to tell you which surgeon to trust. To help you understand what can be established, what still needs to be asked, and what deserves your attention before you decide.

This is my life's work: moving from patient experience to patient intelligence. It costs you nothing, because it belongs to all of us.

File 08 — The Safety Net · From 75 consult rooms, sat on your side

The three questions the room hopes you never ask.

Credentials, anesthesia, and what happens if it goes wrong. I watched 75 consult rooms step around all three. Here’s how you ask them calmly, and get an answer in writing.

01 · The credential question

“Board-certified.” By which board, and can you do this in a hospital?

Credentials are worded for the wall. Hospital privileges are the quiet test: a hospital has reviewed this surgeon for this exact procedure. When the answer is “we only operate here”, that’s worth knowing.

The move

Ask: “Which board certified you, and which hospital lets you do this exact procedure?” Then check the board’s own lookup and call the hospital.

02 · The anesthesia question

Who puts you under, and who watches only you the whole time?

Anesthesia is where the most serious risks sit, and it’s the part of the consult with the fewest words. “Our team handles that” is not a name or a license.

The move

Ask: “Who gives my anesthesia, what is their license, and are they in the room the entire time?” Get the name in writing before the deposit.

03 · The “if it goes wrong” question

If something goes wrong or needs fixing, who treats you, where, and who pays?

The emergency hospital, the revision policy and the malpractice coverage are three answers the consult rarely volunteers. The written quote often skips all three.

The move

Ask: “Which hospital, what does a revision cost me, and do you carry malpractice coverage?” Then search your state medical board lookup: it’s free and takes ten minutes.

Every one of these was dodged in rooms I sat in, so now you ask them first.

The standard we hold for you

Some countries make these the law. Here, you can ask for them.

01 A referral first See your own primary care doctor before any consult. Say your why out loud to someone with nothing to sell.
02 Two consultations Meet the surgeon who will actually operate, twice, before you sign consent. Bring your consult sheet to both.
03 A 7-day waiting period At least a week between signing consent and surgery day. Anyone under 18 deserves far longer.
04 No pressure on money Nobody should push you to finance it, borrow against your 401(k), or earn a bonus for booking you.

Modeled on the strictest cosmetic surgery rules in force today. A practice that says yes to all four is telling you something. So is one that won’t.

File 10 — The Open Lane · Who’s paid to be in the room, and who isn’t

Every player in this industry is paid by someone who profits when you say yes.

WhoHow they’re paidThe move
The review platforms Paid by the surgeons they list. A “verified” badge is a marketplace product. Read the badge. Then ask who bought the listing.
The financiers Interest is the revenue, so your pause is their loss. The financing offer and the surgeon’s marketing often point at the same fast yes. Ask: “Who profits if I say yes today?”
The consult marketplaces Provider-paid fees fill consult rooms. Convenience is the product. Use them to find names. It isn’t investigation. Do both, in that order.
The associations and registers Verification is a floor, written in a language patients don’t read. Use them: they’re the source of truth. Then translate what you find. That’s our job.
The campaigns and the news Funded until the budget ends. Real reform followed their reporting, and then they moved on mid-decision. Take their findings. Then bring them somewhere still here when you’re ready to act.

Nobody in that ledger is paid to be yours. We are.
