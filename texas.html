Texas · State guide

In Texas, the anaesthesia itself has to be registered with the state.

Texas regulates the sedation rather than the room. Any physician giving anaesthesia in an outpatient setting, or operating while someone else gives it, has to register that location with the Texas Medical Board, and the Board inspects them. Four checks, about twenty minutes.

The registration

Office-based anaesthesia is a registration, and the Board inspects it.

Under chapter 192 of the Texas Medical Board rules, each physician who provides anaesthesia services, or who performs a procedure for which anaesthesia services are provided, in an outpatient setting must register that with the Board and pay a fee. The reach is wider than people expect: it includes the use of analgesics and anxiolytics, not only general anaesthesia.

Registration is tied to the physician's biennial registration and identifies the level of anaesthesia provided at each practice site. The Board inspects registered office-based anaesthesia locations. Start at the Texas Medical Board, where you can look up a licence, and ask the practice directly whether this location is registered for office-based anaesthesia and at what level.

The room until you leave

Texas says the equipment stays until you are discharged.

Rule 192.2 requires that all equipment and anaesthesia-related services remain available at the office-based anaesthesia site until the patient is discharged. Not until the procedure ends. Until you leave.

That makes a plain question fair to ask. Who is with me while I wake up, what are they licensed to do, and what equipment is still in the building at that point?

The fifteen-day rule

A transfer that keeps you in hospital over a day has to be reported.

Where a procedure in one of these settings results in an unanticipated, unplanned transport of the patient to a hospital for observation or treatment for more than twenty-four hours, the physician or surgeon must notify the Board in writing within fifteen days.

Physicians working at the deeper end are also held to the standards and guidelines of the American Society of Anesthesiologists. Ask which level of anaesthesia is planned for you, and what happens if you do not wake up the way everyone expects.

The licence

Check the licence and the board separately, because they are separate things.

Look up the exact name printed on your consent form on the Texas Medical Board's licence search, and read the status, what the licence covers, and any disciplinary history on the record.

Then treat board certification as its own question. Ask which board certified them and whether that certification covers the operation being offered, then check that board's own lookup rather than the practice's website.
