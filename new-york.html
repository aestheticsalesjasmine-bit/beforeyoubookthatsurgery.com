New York · State guide

New York publishes the list. If the practice is not on it, it cannot legally operate on you.

Public Health Law 230-d is unusually blunt: a licensee may only perform office-based surgery in a setting that holds full accredited status. The state keeps the list of accredited practices. Checking it takes about ten minutes.

The accreditation

Full accredited status is not optional, and it is not self-declared.

Under Public Health Law section 230-d, a practice in which office-based surgery is performed must obtain and maintain full accredited status from an accrediting agency designated by the Commissioner of Health, and a licensee may only perform office-based surgery in a setting that holds it. The law reaches physicians, physician assistants, specialist assistants, and podiatrists privileged by the State Education Department to perform ankle surgery.

What triggers the requirement is the intended level of sedation, not the name of the drug or the route it is given by. The Department of Health publishes the list of accredited office-based surgery practices in New York State, alongside the law itself, on its office-based surgery pages.

The name on the door

A practice may only call itself office-based surgery if it is accredited.

This is the cheapest tell in the state. A private practice may use the words office-based surgery in its name only when it is accredited to perform it, and practices are told not to use the words facility, centre or clinic in their names at all.

So a name doing heavy lifting is worth a look. Check the list rather than the branding, because the list is the record and the branding is marketing.

The reporting

New York counts the bad outcomes, and the definitions are specific.

A licensee must report adverse events to the Department's Patient Safety Center within three business days. The statute defines them tightly: a patient death within thirty days, an unplanned transfer to a hospital or an emergency department visit within seventy-two hours for reasons related to the surgery, an unscheduled hospital admission or assignment to observation within seventy-two hours lasting longer than twenty-four hours, and any other serious or life-threatening event.

Those four definitions are also a good list of questions. How many of these has this practice reported, and what happened? You may not get an answer. Asking it in front of someone tells you plenty.

The licence

Check the licence and the board separately, because they are separate things.

Verify the exact name printed on your consent form with the State Education Department's Office of the Professions, which licenses physicians in New York, and read the status and any discipline on the record before you go further.

Then treat board certification as its own question. Ask which board certified them and whether that certification covers the operation you are being offered, then check that board's own lookup rather than the practice's website.

Questions

New York, answered plainly.

Does a New York cosmetic surgery office have to be accredited?

Yes, where office-based surgery as defined by Public Health Law 230-d is performed. The practice must obtain and maintain full accredited status from an accrediting agency designated by the Commissioner of Health, and a licensee may only perform office-based surgery in a setting holding that status. What triggers it is the intended level of sedation, not the specific medication or route.

How do I check whether a New York practice is accredited?

The New York State Department of Health publishes the list of accredited office-based surgery practices on its office-based surgery pages at health.ny.gov. Check the practice against the list rather than relying on its website.

What does it mean if a practice calls itself an office-based surgery centre?

A private practice may only use office-based surgery in its name if it is accredited to perform it, and practices are directed not to use the words facility, centre or clinic in their names. A name that implies more than the accreditation supports is worth checking against the state's list.

What has to be reported to the state after office-based surgery in New York?

Licensees must report adverse events to the Department of Health's Patient Safety Center within three business days. Reportable events include a patient death within thirty days, an unplanned hospital transfer or emergency department visit within seventy-two hours related to the surgery, an unscheduled admission or observation stay within seventy-two hours lasting over twenty-four hours, and any other serious or life-threatening event.
